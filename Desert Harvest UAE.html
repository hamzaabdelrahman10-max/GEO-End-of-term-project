Vertical farm
Bustanica, Dubai		 Over 1 million kg of leafy greens a year, 3,000 kg a day, 95% less water, no pesticides, harvested close to where it is eaten. 1,000 tonnes a year is about 0.006% of the 16.9 million tonnes of food imported in 2022. It grows leafy greens, not the crops that use most groundwater. Cooling and lighting need electricity.
High-tech greenhouses
Al Ain		 An 11 to 12 hectare controlled-environment site for tomatoes and other produce, one of the early large-scale projects in the national strategy. Still a very small share of farmland. High set-up costs mean it depends on grants and investment.
Salt-tolerant crops
ICBA, Dubai		 Five quinoa varieties found from about 1,200 tested. Salicornia reached 3 tonnes of seed per hectare using seawater that first passed through fish tanks. Seed bank of about 14,000 samples. Output is at research scale. ICBA reports that getting people to buy and eat the crops is the harder problem.
National policy
Strategy 2051		 Launched in 2018 with 38 initiatives, aiming for first place in the Global Food Security Index by 2051. Around 70% of food imports pass through the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, so supply routes stay exposed.

Part 3

Improve it: three geographical gaps

Reading the map and the numbers together shows where the current approach leaves something out.

Gap 1 · Place

The sites sit in one corridor

The solutions I mapped are all in the Dubai to Al Ain corridor. The Liwa aquifer in the Western Region holds fossil water from the last Ice Age and has no site on the map.

Gap 2 · Crop

Leafy greens, not the big water users

Most groundwater goes to farms, forestry and landscaping, and date palms alone take about a third of what major crops use. New farms mostly grow salad.

Gap 3 · Resource

Salty water goes unused

ICBA describes saline water left after desalination as a resource that could be used. Today it is mostly a disposal problem, and farmers keep pumping fresh groundwater.

My recommendation

Brine-to-Biomass hubs

Build a pilot farm next to a coastal desalination plant that grows salt-tolerant animal feed using seawater and reject brine, with fish tanks in the water loop and solar power. The feed replaces fodder grown with groundwater.

Part 4

Justify it: why, where and how it helps

Why it is needed

  • Groundwater is running down. Recharge is about 4% of use, and levels fall up to 5 m a year in the worst areas.
  • It builds on proven work. ICBA has already grown salicornia on seawater and trialled forage on saline water.
  • There is demand. A pilot in Al Ain is already testing vertical-farmed animal feed beside livestock farms.

Where it should go

  • Stage 1: Taweelah coast, between Abu Dhabi and Dubai. It has coastal water infrastructure and road links to both emirates.
  • Stage 2: Western Region coast. Supply feed to farms around Liwa to take pressure off the fossil aquifer.
  • Why not inland? Seawater and brine are cheapest to use where they are produced.

How it improves sustainability

  • Water: uses saline water instead of fresh groundwater.
  • Waste: gives brine a second use before disposal.
  • Food security: adds local feed, which supports local meat and dairy.
  • Energy: solar power keeps the pilot low-carbon.

Risks and safeguards

  • Salt leaking into soil and aquifers: use lined beds and monitor wells.
  • Low demand: sign feed contracts with livestock farms before building.
  • Cost: start with a small pilot and measure before scaling.

How to measure success

  • Cubic metres of groundwater saved against fodder grown the usual way.
  • Tonnes of feed produced per hectare.
  • Share of brine reused instead of discharged.
  • Groundwater levels in nearby monitoring wells.

The hub is a proposal. Its design details are my own suggestions built on the cited research, not measured results.
